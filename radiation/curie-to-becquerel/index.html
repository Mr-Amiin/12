About Converting Curies to Becquerels

Curie and Becquerel both belong to the radiation-measurement family, though radiation quantities fall into genuinely distinct categories: absorbed dose (gray, rad) measures energy deposited in matter, equivalent dose (sievert, rem) weights that for biological harm, and activity (becquerel, curie) measures how often a radioactive source decays — this page only converts between units of the same kind. Curie is typically associated with still widely used in the United States alongside the SI becquerel, while Becquerel is typically associated with universal — SI derived unit used worldwide.

Formula

becquerels = curies × 37000000000

This factor comes from each unit's defined relationship to the category's base unit: 1 curie equals 37000000000 base units, and 1 becquerel equals 1 base unit, so dividing one by the other gives the direct curie-to-becquerel factor of 37000000000.

Simple example

1 Ci × 37000000000 = 37,000,000,000 Bq

Real-world example

Understanding the Curie

Curie (Ci) measures radioactivity (activity). Originally based on the activity of one gram of radium-226; now fixed at exactly 3.7 × 10¹⁰ decays per second. It is classified as a non-SI unit, still common in the US.

Where it's used: Still widely used in the United States alongside the SI becquerel

Curie vs. becquerel. Both measure radioactive activity (decays per second), but the becquerel is the modern SI unit (1 decay/second) while the curie is the older, much larger unit (37 billion decays/second) still common in US medical and regulatory contexts.

Uses of the Curie today

  • Nuclear medicine dosing in the US
  • Radioactive source labeling
  • Historical and US regulatory radiation-activity reporting

History of the Curie

The curie is named after Marie Curie (1867–1934) and Pierre Curie (1859–1906), the physicists who coined the term 'radioactivity,' discovered the elements polonium and radium, and won the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics for their research — Marie Curie later won a second Nobel Prize, in Chemistry, in 1911.

Historical origin. Curie was not the invention of a single named person; it developed through the process described above.

Understanding the Becquerel

Becquerel (Bq) measures radioactivity (activity). The SI derived unit of radioactive activity, equal to exactly one radioactive decay per second. It is classified as a SI derived unit.

Where it's used: Universal — SI derived unit used worldwide

Uses of the Becquerel today

  • International radiation safety and environmental monitoring
  • Nuclear medicine dosing outside the US
  • Food and water radioactivity contamination limits

History of the Becquerel

The becquerel is named after Henri Becquerel (1852–1908), the French physicist who discovered radioactivity in 1896 when he found that uranium salts emitted radiation spontaneously, without any external energy source — sharing the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics with Marie and Pierre Curie for this work.

Historical origin. Becquerel was not the invention of a single named person; it developed through the process described above.
