Understanding the Gram

Gram (g) measures mass. A gram is one-thousandth of a kilogram, the SI base unit of mass. It is classified as a SI derived (metric prefix) unit.

Where it's used: Standard small-mass unit worldwide

Uses of the Gram today

  • Cooking and nutrition labeling worldwide (including in the US, alongside ounces)
  • Precious metal and small-item weighing
  • Scientific and laboratory measurement

History of the Gram

Originally defined in 1795 as the mass of one cubic centimeter of water, the gram follows directly from the kilogram (1/1000 of it) under the modern SI system, inheriting the kilogram's various historical and current definitions.

Historical origin. Gram was not the invention of a single named person; it developed through the process described above.

Understanding the Pound

Pound (lb) measures mass. The avoirdupois pound is legally defined as exactly 0.45359237 kilograms, fixed by international treaty in 1959. It is classified as a US/imperial customary unit.

Where it's used: United States (primary mass/weight unit) United Kingdom (informal and some retail use) Various Caribbean and Pacific territories with historical UK/US ties

Pound-mass vs. pound-force. This page converts pound-mass (lbm), a unit of mass. Pound-force (lbf) is a separate unit measuring the force gravity exerts on one pound-mass, used in engineering contexts like pounds per square inch.

Uses of the Pound today

  • Body weight and food retail packaging in the United States
  • Postal, parcel, and freight weight in US shipping and logistics
  • Pound-force (lbf) and its derivatives (psi, foot-pounds) in US engineering and aerospace

History of the Pound

Unlike the kilogram's single documented birth in revolutionary France, the pound's history is a long, layered narrowing of competing regional standards. The avoirdupois pound — ancestor of today's standard — emerged among London merchants in the early 14th century (recorded use around 1303), used for weighing wool and other bulk goods under the system merchants called 'avoir de pois,' Old French for 'goods of weight.' Its 16-ounce structure suited large-scale trade better than the 12-ounce troy pound used for precious metals.

Wool merchants used a related 'wool pound' of about 6,992 grains — close enough to 7,000 that Elizabeth I's government formally fixed the avoirdupois pound at exactly 7,000 grains in 1588, the value still used today. Britain and the US then refined their physical standards separately over the following centuries, leaving small discrepancies between them.

Those discrepancies were resolved on 1 July 1959, when the US, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and South Africa signed the International Yard and Pound Agreement, fixing the pound at exactly 0.45359237 kg and the yard at exactly 0.9144 m. The US National Bureau of Standards (now NIST) adopted the value immediately; the UK followed via its Weights and Measures Act 1963 — the value used by virtually every country today.

Historical origin. Pound was not the invention of a single named person; it developed through the process described above.
