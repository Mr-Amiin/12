About Converting Centimeters to Angstroms

Centimeter and Angstrom both measure length. Centimeter is typically associated with standard small-length unit worldwide except the US, while Angstrom is typically associated with widely used internationally in chemistry, crystallography, and atomic/molecular physics.

Formula

angstroms = centimeters × 100000000

This factor comes from each unit's defined relationship to the category's base unit: 1 centimeter equals 0.01 base units, and 1 angstrom equals 1e-10 base units, so dividing one by the other gives the direct centimeter-to-angstrom factor of 100000000.

Simple example

1 cm × 100000000 = 100,000,000 A

1 centimeter = 100,000,000 angstroms.

Real-world example

Understanding the Centimeter

Centimeter (cm) measures distance. A centimeter is one-hundredth of a meter. It is classified as a SI derived (metric prefix) unit.

Where it's used: Standard small-length unit worldwide except the US

Uses of the Centimeter today

  • Everyday small-object and body measurement (height, clothing sizes) outside the US
  • Construction and manufacturing tolerances internationally

History of the Centimeter

The centimeter follows directly from the meter via the 'centi-' SI prefix (10⁻²), introduced with the metric system in the 1790s.

Historical origin. Centimeter was not the invention of a single named person; it developed through the process described above.

Understanding the Angstrom

Angstrom (A) measures length. Equal to exactly 10⁻¹⁰ meters (0.1 nanometers) — a scale convenient for describing atomic radii, bond lengths, and crystal structures. It is classified as a non-SI unit, still common in chemistry and crystallography.

Where it's used: Widely used internationally in chemistry, crystallography, and atomic/molecular physics

Uses of the Angstrom today

  • Atomic and ionic radii in chemistry
  • Chemical bond-length measurements
  • X-ray crystallography and wavelength measurements

History of the Angstrom

The angstrom is named after Anders Jonas Ångström (1814–1874), a Swedish physicist and one of the founders of spectroscopy, who used the unit in his 1868 atlas of the solar spectrum.

Historical origin. Angstrom was not the invention of a single named person; it developed through the process described above.
