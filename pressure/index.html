Pressure

Convert common pressure units used in engineering, weather, tires, and industrial work.

What Pressure conversions measure

Pressure conversions cover units such as pascals, PSI, and bar. Converting between them lets you move between the different measurement systems and conventions used across regions and industries.

Common real-world use

Common real-world uses include tire pressure, hydraulics and pneumatics, meteorology, and industrial engineering.

Choosing a unit

Pick the unit your source data, audience, or regional convention already uses, and convert once at the start of a calculation rather than mixing systems partway through.

About Pressure Conversion

Pressure measures force distributed over an area, and it shows up in contexts as different as car tire inflation, weather forecasting, and industrial hydraulics, each of which tends to favor its own unit. The pascal is the SI unit, but it's small enough that everyday pressures are often expressed in kilopascals. In the United States, pounds per square inch (psi) is the standard for tire pressure and many mechanical specifications, while bar and its subunit millibar are widely used in Europe and in meteorology for atmospheric pressure.

Additional pressure units persist in specific fields: atmospheres (atm) reference standard sea-level air pressure, and inches or millimeters of mercury (inHg, mmHg) trace back to mercury barometers historically used to measure atmospheric and blood pressure. This converter covers the common engineering, meteorological, and medical pressure units, pascal, kilopascal, bar, psi, and atmosphere, using precise standard definitions so a converted tire pressure, weather reading, or hydraulic specification stays accurate.

Because different industries and instruments default to different pressure units, accurate conversion is often a practical necessity rather than a convenience: a US-made gauge reading psi needs translating for a European specification in bar, and a weather report in millibars needs context for someone used to thinking in inches of mercury. Getting pressure conversions right matters especially in safety-critical contexts, like tire inflation, scuba diving depth calculations, or industrial equipment ratings, where an incorrect pressure reading could have real consequences.

When converting pressure for something safety-relevant, like tire inflation or a pressure vessel rating, it's worth double-checking the converted figure against the equipment manufacturer's specified range rather than relying on a single conversion alone, since a unit mix-up (psi versus bar, for instance) could otherwise lead to significant over- or under-pressurization. It also helps to remember that a bar is very close in size to one standard atmosphere, which makes it a useful mental anchor when sanity-checking a converted pressure value against everyday sea-level conditions.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between psi and bar?

Psi (pounds per square inch) is a US customary pressure unit, while bar is a metric unit close to standard atmospheric pressure. One bar equals about 14.5 psi, so bar is the larger unit.

Why is atmospheric pressure defined as 101,325 pascals?

This value, 1 standard atmosphere, was chosen as a reference approximating average air pressure at sea level, and it's used as a baseline in meteorology, aviation, and engineering calculations.

How do I convert car tire pressure between psi and bar?

Multiply psi by 0.0689476 to get bar, or multiply bar by 14.5038 to get psi. Most tire pressure gauges and pumps display both units for convenience.

What does 'gauge pressure' versus 'absolute pressure' mean?

Gauge pressure measures pressure relative to atmospheric pressure (what a tire gauge shows), while absolute pressure measures relative to a perfect vacuum (zero pressure). Absolute pressure equals gauge pressure plus atmospheric pressure.

Why do weather forecasts use millibars or hectopascals?

A millibar (equal to a hectopascal) is a convenient unit size for expressing the small atmospheric pressure variations that drive weather systems, since standard sea-level pressure is about 1,013 millibars, a number meteorologists find easy to track and compare.

When Should I Use Each Pressure Unit?

  • Pascal — Used in scientific and international engineering contexts as the SI pressure unit.
  • PSI — Used in the US for tire pressure and many mechanical specifications.
  • Bar — Used across Europe for tire pressure, industrial equipment, and diving gear.
  • Millibar / hectopascal — Used in meteorology to report atmospheric pressure and weather systems.

Common Mistakes

  • ❌ Confusing hectopascals with millibars, which are actually numerically identical and often interchangeable.
  • ❌ Mixing up psi and bar, off by roughly a factor of 14.5.
  • ❌ Assuming pressure is the same as force, when pressure is force distributed over an area.
  • ❌ Using gauge pressure and absolute pressure interchangeably, which differ by atmospheric pressure.

History

The pascal was adopted as the SI unit of pressure in 1971, named after Blaise Pascal, whose 17th-century experiments with barometers and fluid pressure laid the groundwork for the field. Because a single pascal is a tiny amount of pressure, roughly the weight of a piece of paper spread over a square meter, most everyday pressures are expressed in kilopascals or, in weather forecasting, hectopascals. The bar, though not an official SI unit, remains widely used across Europe because it was defined to be close to standard atmospheric pressure, making it an intuitive reference point. Psi persisted in the US largely through the automotive and manufacturing industries, which standardized around imperial units long before pascal-based SI pressure became the international scientific norm.
