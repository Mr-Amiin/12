Understanding the Celsius

Celsius (C) measures temperature. Celsius is defined relative to the Kelvin scale, with 0 °C set at 273.15 K. It is classified as a SI-derived temperature scale.

Where it's used: Standard temperature scale in nearly every country except the United States

Uses of the Celsius today

  • Weather forecasting and climate reporting worldwide
  • Scientific and laboratory temperature measurement (alongside Kelvin)
  • Cooking and food safety in most countries outside the US

History of the Celsius

Swedish astronomer Anders Celsius proposed a 100-point temperature scale in 1742, but his original scale ran in the opposite direction, with 0° at water's boiling point and 100° at freezing.

The scale was reversed to its modern orientation shortly after his death, commonly attributed to instrument maker Daniel Ekström and naturalist Carl Linnaeus around 1744-1745.

The scale was renamed from 'centigrade' to 'Celsius' by international agreement in 1948, and it is now formally defined as the Kelvin scale shifted by 273.15, rather than independently by the freezing and boiling points of water.

Historical origin. Celsius was not the invention of a single named person; it developed through the process described above.
