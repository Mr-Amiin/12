About Converting Quarts to Liters

Quart and Liter both show up in recipes, and converting between them matters whenever a recipe from one country's cookbook needs to be followed with another country's measuring tools. Quart is typically associated with united States (cooking, dairy, motor oil packaging), while Liter is typically associated with standard volume unit for liquids worldwide, including in the US for beverages and fuel labeling in some contexts.

Understanding the Quart

Quart (qt) measures volume. The US liquid quart is exactly one-quarter of a US gallon: 0.946352946 liters. It is classified as a US customary unit.

Where it's used: United States (cooking, dairy, motor oil packaging)

Uses of the Quart today

  • US recipe measurement (larger liquid volumes)
  • US motor oil and automotive fluid packaging
  • US dairy and beverage packaging (quart containers)

History of the Quart

The quart ('quarter' of a gallon) follows the same English wine-gallon lineage as the US gallon, retained by the US after England moved to a different imperial standard in 1824.

Historical origin. Quart was not the invention of a single named person; it developed through the process described above.

Understanding the Liter

Liter (L) measures volume. A liter is defined as exactly one cubic decimeter (0.001 cubic meters). It is classified as a Non-SI metric unit accepted for use with SI.

Where it's used: Standard volume unit for liquids worldwide, including in the US for beverages and fuel labeling in some contexts

Uses of the Liter today

  • Beverage, fuel, and liquid product packaging worldwide
  • Cooking and recipe measurement in metric countries
  • Laboratory and scientific volume measurement

History of the Liter

The liter was introduced with the French metric system in 1795, originally defined as the volume of one kilogram of pure water at its freezing point — tying volume, mass, and the meter together.

Between 1901 and 1964, the liter was briefly redefined as the volume of exactly one kilogram of water at its maximum density (about 4 °C), which made it very slightly larger than a cubic decimeter. It was redefined back to exactly one cubic decimeter in 1964, the definition still used today.

Historical origin. Liter was not the invention of a single named person; it developed through the process described above.
