About Converting Gallons to Zeptoliters

Gallon and Zeptoliter appear side by side at fuel pumps and on beverage labels, since the US measures liquids in gallons while most of the world uses liters. Gallon is most often used for fuel pump and vehicle fuel-economy measurement in the us.

Understanding the Gallon

Gallon (gal) measures volume. The US liquid gallon is defined as exactly 231 cubic inches, equal to 3.785411784 liters. It is classified as a US customary unit.

Where it's used: United States (fuel, beverage, and liquid measurement)

US gallon vs. imperial gallon. The US liquid gallon (3.785411784 L) and the British imperial gallon (4.54609 L) are different units with the same name; always confirm which one a source is using.

Uses of the Gallon today

  • Fuel pump and vehicle fuel-economy measurement in the US
  • Beverage and liquid product packaging in the US
  • Cooking and recipe measurement in US customary units

History of the Gallon

The US gallon descends from the English wine gallon of 231 cubic inches, a standard used for measuring wine in medieval and early modern England, which the American colonies inherited and retained even after England moved to a different imperial gallon in 1824.

England's 1824 Weights and Measures Act replaced the old wine gallon with a new imperial gallon (based on 10 pounds of water), but the US kept the older 231-cubic-inch definition — which is why the US gallon (3.785 L) and the imperial gallon (4.546 L) differ by about 20% today.

Historical origin. Gallon was not the invention of a single named person; it developed through the process described above.
