About Converting Liters to Imperial gallons

Liter and Imperial gallon appear side by side at fuel pumps and on beverage labels, since the US measures liquids in gallons while most of the world uses liters. Liter is typically associated with standard volume unit for liquids worldwide, including in the US for beverages and fuel labeling in some contexts, while Imperial gallon is typically associated with united Kingdom, Canada (some contexts), and other Commonwealth countries.

Understanding the Liter

Liter (L) measures volume. A liter is defined as exactly one cubic decimeter (0.001 cubic meters). It is classified as a Non-SI metric unit accepted for use with SI.

Where it's used: Standard volume unit for liquids worldwide, including in the US for beverages and fuel labeling in some contexts

Uses of the Liter today

  • Beverage, fuel, and liquid product packaging worldwide
  • Cooking and recipe measurement in metric countries
  • Laboratory and scientific volume measurement

History of the Liter

The liter was introduced with the French metric system in 1795, originally defined as the volume of one kilogram of pure water at its freezing point — tying volume, mass, and the meter together.

Between 1901 and 1964, the liter was briefly redefined as the volume of exactly one kilogram of water at its maximum density (about 4 °C), which made it very slightly larger than a cubic decimeter. It was redefined back to exactly one cubic decimeter in 1964, the definition still used today.

Historical origin. Liter was not the invention of a single named person; it developed through the process described above.

Understanding the Imperial gallon

Imperial gallon (imp gal) measures volume. The imperial gallon is defined as exactly 4.54609 liters, about 20% larger than the US gallon. It is classified as a UK/imperial customary unit.

Where it's used: United Kingdom, Canada (some contexts), and other Commonwealth countries

Imperial gallon vs. US gallon. Britain's 1824 Weights and Measures Act defined a new gallon based on 10 pounds of water, replacing the old wine gallon the US had already adopted — leaving the US (3.785 L) and imperial (4.546 L) gallons about 20% apart today.

Uses of the Imperial gallon today

  • UK fuel and beverage measurement (historically; UK now primarily uses liters for retail)
  • Commonwealth country liquid measurement in some contexts

History of the Imperial gallon

The imperial gallon was created by Britain's Weights and Measures Act of 1824, defined as the volume of 10 pounds of water, replacing several inconsistent older English gallon definitions (wine gallon, ale gallon, corn gallon) with one standard.

Historical origin. Imperial gallon was not the invention of a single named person; it developed through the process described above.
