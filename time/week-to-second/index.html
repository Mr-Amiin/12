About Converting Weeks to Seconds

Weeks and Seconds are both everyday units of duration, and converting between them is one of the most routine calculations there is — scheduling, project planning, and billing all depend on it. Weeks is most often used for work schedules and calendars worldwide.

Formula

seconds = weeks × 604800

This factor comes from each unit's defined relationship to the category's base unit: 1 weeks equals 604800 base units, and 1 seconds equals 1 base unit, so dividing one by the other gives the direct weeks-to-seconds factor of 604800.

Simple example

1 wk × 604800 = 604,800 s

1 weeks = 604,800 seconds.

Real-world example

24 wk × 604800 = 14,515,200 s

24 weeks = 14,515,200 seconds.

Understanding the Weeks

Weeks (wk) measures time. A week is 7 days, a cycle with no astronomical basis, historically linked to religious and market traditions. It is classified as a Conventional (non-SI) time unit.

Where it's used: Universal

Uses of the Weeks today

  • Work schedules and calendars worldwide
  • Project planning and scheduling
  • Pregnancy and medical timelines

History of the Weeks

The seven-day week likely originated in ancient Babylon, possibly tied to the seven visible celestial bodies known to ancient astronomers (Sun, Moon, and five planets), and spread through Judaism, then Christianity and the Roman Empire, into near-universal modern use despite having no basis in astronomical cycles the way the day, month, or year do.

Historical origin. Weeks was not the invention of a single named person; it developed through the process described above.

Understanding the Seconds

Seconds (s) measures time. The second is the SI base unit of time, currently defined via a fixed number of cesium atomic transitions. It is classified as a SI base unit.

Where it's used: Universal

Uses of the Seconds today

  • Fundamental unit for all timekeeping and scientific measurement
  • Basis for derived units like speed (m/s) and frequency (Hz)

History of the Seconds

The second originated from dividing the day into 24 hours, each into 60 minutes, each into 60 seconds — a sexagesimal (base-60) system inherited from Babylonian astronomy. Since 1967, the second has been defined atomically, as exactly 9,192,631,770 periods of radiation from a cesium-133 transition, decoupling timekeeping from the Earth's slightly irregular rotation.

Historical origin. Seconds was not the invention of a single named person; it developed through the process described above.
