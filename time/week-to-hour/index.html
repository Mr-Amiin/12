About Converting Weeks to Hours

Weeks and Hours are both everyday units of duration, and converting between them is one of the most routine calculations there is — scheduling, project planning, and billing all depend on it. Weeks is most often used for work schedules and calendars worldwide.

Formula

hours = weeks × 168

This factor comes from each unit's defined relationship to the category's base unit: 1 weeks equals 604800 base units, and 1 hours equals 3600 base units, so dividing one by the other gives the direct weeks-to-hours factor of 168.

Simple example

1 wk × 168 = 168 h

1 weeks = 168 hours.

Real-world example

24 wk × 168 = 4,032 h

24 weeks = 4,032 hours.

Understanding the Weeks

Weeks (wk) measures time. A week is 7 days, a cycle with no astronomical basis, historically linked to religious and market traditions. It is classified as a Conventional (non-SI) time unit.

Where it's used: Universal

Uses of the Weeks today

  • Work schedules and calendars worldwide
  • Project planning and scheduling
  • Pregnancy and medical timelines

History of the Weeks

The seven-day week likely originated in ancient Babylon, possibly tied to the seven visible celestial bodies known to ancient astronomers (Sun, Moon, and five planets), and spread through Judaism, then Christianity and the Roman Empire, into near-universal modern use despite having no basis in astronomical cycles the way the day, month, or year do.

Historical origin. Weeks was not the invention of a single named person; it developed through the process described above.

Understanding the Hours

Hours (h) measures time. An hour is 60 minutes (3,600 seconds); dividing the day into 24 hours dates to ancient Egyptian timekeeping. It is classified as a Non-SI unit accepted for use with SI.

Where it's used: Universal

Uses of the Hours today

  • Work schedules and everyday timekeeping worldwide
  • Travel time and duration planning
  • Billing (hourly rates, parking, rentals)

History of the Hours

The 24-hour day traces to ancient Egypt, which divided daytime and nighttime into 12 parts each using sundials and star-based timekeeping; the modern fixed-length hour (rather than daylight-proportional 'temporal hours') became standard with the spread of mechanical clocks in medieval Europe.

Historical origin. Hours was not the invention of a single named person; it developed through the process described above.
