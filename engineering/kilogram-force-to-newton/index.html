About Converting Kilogram-forces to Newtons

Kilogram-force and Newton both measure a quantity used in structural or mechanical engineering — commonly stress, pressure, force, torque, power, or energy — where converting between the unit conventions used in different regions, industries, or equipment specifications is a routine part of design work. Kilogram-force is typically associated with still encountered in some older European and Asian engineering specifications and torque-related equipment, though official use has been phased out in favor of the newton, while Newton is typically associated with standard scientific and engineering force unit worldwide.

Understanding the Kilogram-force

Kilogram-force (kgf) measures force. Defined as the force exerted by standard gravity on a mass of one kilogram — exactly 9.80665 newtons. It is classified as a non-SI unit, deprecated but still encountered.

Where it's used: Still encountered in some older European and Asian engineering specifications and torque-related equipment, though official use has been phased out in favor of the newton

Kilogram-force vs. newton. The newton is the modern SI unit of force; kilogram-force is an older, gravity-dependent unit still occasionally seen on legacy equipment. 1 kgf equals exactly 9.80665 N.

Uses of the Kilogram-force today

  • Appears on some older or legacy engineering and mechanical specifications, particularly torque wrenches and spring-force ratings, predating wider adoption of the newton

Understanding the Newton

Newton (N) measures force. The newton is the SI derived unit of force: the force needed to accelerate a 1 kg mass at 1 meter per second squared. It is classified as a SI derived unit.

Where it's used: Standard scientific and engineering force unit worldwide

Uses of the Newton today

  • Physics and engineering force calculations
  • Material strength and structural engineering specifications
  • Product specifications (e.g. spring force, adhesive strength)

History of the Newton

The newton is named after Isaac Newton in recognition of his laws of motion, which define the relationship between force, mass, and acceleration that the unit embodies. It was formally adopted as the SI derived unit of force as part of the broader 20th-century SI system.

Historical origin. Newton was not the invention of a single named person; it developed through the process described above.
