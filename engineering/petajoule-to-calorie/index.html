Understanding the Calorie

Calorie (cal) measures energy. The thermochemical calorie used in this converter is defined as exactly 4.184 joules. It is classified as a Non-SI unit (limited use with SI).

Where it's used: Nutrition labeling in the US and several other countries still commonly uses the kilocalorie (as 'Calorie')

Calorie vs. Calorie (kilocalorie). Food labels' capitalized 'Calorie' is actually one kilocalorie (1,000 small calories) — a long-standing labeling convention that causes frequent confusion.

Uses of the Calorie today

  • Food energy labeling (as kilocalories, often written 'Calories')
  • Historical chemistry and thermodynamics contexts

History of the Calorie

The calorie was originally defined in the early 19th century as the heat needed to raise one gram of water by one degree Celsius, but different scientific traditions defined it at slightly different reference temperatures, producing several close but non-identical 'calorie' values historically (15 °C calorie, mean calorie, thermochemical calorie).

To resolve this ambiguity, the thermochemical calorie was fixed by convention at exactly 4.184 joules; the very similar International Table calorie is fixed at exactly 4.1868 joules. The nutritional 'Calorie' seen on food labels is actually a kilocalorie (1,000 calories).

Historical origin. Calorie was not the invention of a single named person; it developed through the process described above.
