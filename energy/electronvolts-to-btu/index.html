About Converting Electronvolts to BTU

Electronvolt and BTU both measure energy, and the conversion is especially useful for comparing nutrition labels (kilocalories) against scientific energy figures (joules). Electronvolt is typically associated with standard unit throughout particle physics, atomic physics, and chemistry worldwide, while BTU is typically associated with united States (HVAC, heating, and appliance energy ratings).

btu = electronvolts × 1.51857e-22

This factor comes from each unit's defined relationship to the category's base unit: 1 electronvolt equals 1.602177e-19 base units, and 1 btu equals 1055.05585262 base units, so dividing one by the other gives the direct electronvolt-to-btu factor of 1.51857e-22.

Understanding the Electronvolt

Electronvolt (eV) measures energy. The energy gained by a single electron accelerated through an electric potential difference of one volt — a very small amount of energy, exactly 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ joules. It is classified as a non-SI unit accepted for use with the SI, common in physics.

Where it's used: Standard unit throughout particle physics, atomic physics, and chemistry worldwide

Uses of the Electronvolt today

  • Particle physics (particle accelerator energies are often quoted in GeV or TeV)
  • Atomic and molecular energy levels
  • Semiconductor band-gap energies

Understanding the BTU

BTU (BTU) measures energy. A British thermal unit is defined as exactly 1,055.05585262 joules (the International Table BTU). It is classified as a US customary unit.

Where it's used: United States (HVAC, heating, and appliance energy ratings)

Uses of the BTU today

  • Air conditioner and furnace capacity ratings in the US
  • Water heater and appliance energy specifications
  • US energy industry (natural gas is often billed via BTU content)

History of the BTU

The BTU originated in 19th-century steam engineering as the heat needed to raise one pound of water by one degree Fahrenheit, paralleling the calorie's metric definition. Several slightly different BTU values existed historically before the 'International Table' BTU was standardized at exactly 1,055.05585262 joules.

Historical origin. BTU was not the invention of a single named person; it developed through the process described above.
